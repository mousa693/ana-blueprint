# How Agencies Can Onboard a New Client's Brand Voice into AI (Without a Week of Prompt-Tuning)

The contract is signed and the kickoff call went well. Now someone at the agency has to get the client's voice into the AI tools before the first content batch is due.

On most teams that means a long "brand voice prompt" pasted into a chat window, a shared doc of example captions, and two rounds of drafts that sound almost right. The first month gets eaten by calibration.

This guide sets out a repeatable onboarding process that replaces calibration with context. It works with any AI stack. Where it helps, we show how it runs in Fuzzbucket, which builds a reusable **Brand Brain** from a client's website and social profiles.

## Why brand voice breaks when agencies add AI

Brand voice rarely fails because the model is bad. It fails for three operational reasons:

1. **The voice was never written down.** Clients often can't describe their own voice. They know it when they see it. If the agency has nothing written down to point to, every draft becomes a matter of taste.
2. **Context lives in people, not systems.** The account manager knows the client hates exclamation marks and never says "cheap". The AI doesn't, and neither does the freelancer covering the account next month.
3. **Prompts don't scale across clients.** A 600-word prompt that works for one client turns into twelve slightly different prompts across twelve clients, kept in twelve places. Voice drifts quietly.

What fixes this is a structured, reusable brand profile that every piece of content draws from, kept per client. Better prompts won't do it.

## Step 1: Collect the right source material before kickoff

AI can only reflect what you give it. Ask the client for these before the kickoff call, not after:

- **Official website URL** (homepage, about page, key product or service pages)
- **Official social profiles** (Instagram, Facebook Page, Threads, plus any others they use)
- **3–5 pieces of content they love**, from themselves or anyone else, with one sentence on *why*
- **3 pieces they'd never publish**, and why
- **Words and claims that are off-limits** (legal, regulatory or simply disliked)
- **Primary audience in one sentence**, e.g. "first-time homebuyers in Manchester, 28–40"
- **Current goal for the next 90 days** (launch, awareness, bookings, sales)

The "never publish" list is the most underrated input. Negative examples define a voice faster than positive ones.

## Step 2: Turn public presence into a structured brand profile

This is where most agencies lose time, because it's manual. Someone reads the website, skims the Instagram grid, and writes a tone summary.

An AI brand-intake step can do the first pass. In Fuzzbucket you choose **Import my existing brand** and share an official website or social link. The assistant then:

- visits the sources and maps the relevant pages
- proposes a **Brand Profile** covering identity, visual guidance, audience and public presence
- suggests visual directions, palettes, logo options, **Products** and brand media for you to accept or reject
- asks clarification questions where the evidence is thin

The output becomes part of the client's Brand Brain. That's durable context (strategy, plans, Knowledge documents and memory) that every later campaign and asset draws on. You don't paste it into a prompt each time. (See the docs: [Set up your first brand](https://fuzzbucket.ai/docs/getting-started/set-up-your-first-brand).)

Whichever tool you use, the principle is the same: **extract first, then edit.** Reviewing a draft profile is far faster than writing one from scratch.

## Step 3: Review the profile like an editor, not a passenger

Automated intake will be mostly right, and the parts that are wrong matter. Run every new brand profile through this review before any content is generated:

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Audience** | Is it specific enough to change word choice? "Small businesses" is too vague. "Independent café owners" works. |
| **Tone** | Are there 3–5 adjectives *plus* a "not this" for each? ("Confident, not arrogant.") |
| **Claims** | Are any product claims, prices or guarantees inferred rather than stated by the client? Delete them. |
| **Visuals** | Do the palette and logo match the current brand, not an old cached version? |
| **Products** | Are the products and services current, named correctly, and complete? |
| **Off-limits** | Is the "never say" list captured somewhere the AI will actually read? |

In Fuzzbucket, only keep the Brand Profile sections, Products and media that accurately represent the brand before you confirm. Anything you add later, such as a voice guide, a list of banned phrases or a legal disclaimer, can go into the **Knowledge** library as a Markdown document that the agents share.

## Step 4: Add the "tribal knowledge" layer

Public sources only tell you how the brand presents itself. The client's preferences, and the things they've learned the hard way, live elsewhere. Write a short Knowledge document per client with:

- **Voice rules**, e.g. "UK spelling. No emojis in Facebook posts. One emoji maximum on Instagram."
- **Phrases to use and avoid**, e.g. "Say 'members', never 'customers'."
- **Proof points** the client has approved: real numbers, testimonials, awards
- **Competitor names** and how (or whether) to reference them
- **Seasonal or regulatory constraints**, e.g. financial promotions rules or alcohol advertising restrictions

Keep it to one page. If it's longer than that, nobody will maintain it, human or AI.

## Step 5: Run a calibration batch, not a campaign

Don't make the first AI output a live campaign. Generate a small calibration set and get the client to react to it:

- 3 Instagram captions in different lengths
- 2 Facebook posts
- 2 Threads posts (shorter, more conversational)
- 2–3 visuals in the proposed style

Ask the client to mark each one **"sounds like us"**, **"close"** or **"not us"**, and to give one reason. Feed the reasons straight back into the Knowledge document. One round of this usually settles more than a week of back-and-forth over prompts.

In Fuzzbucket, a calibration batch can live in a workspace for that brand, with chat, Canvas and creative nodes side by side. That makes it easy to revise a single caption or image without starting over.

## Step 6: Move from assets to a plan

Once the voice is calibrated, stop producing one-off assets and build a plan. A good AI-assisted plan gives you:

- one **objective** and KPI for the period
- the **channels** and the role of each one
- a **core message** and supporting themes
- a **calendar** of entries with next actions

Fuzzbucket's marketing plans turn reviewed brand context into an objective, plan, checklist, calendar entries and next actions ([docs](https://fuzzbucket.ai/docs/planning/marketing-plans-and-suggestions)). Creative production then follows the plan rather than happening in isolation.

## Step 7: Close the loop with performance

Voice onboarding isn't finished at kickoff. Once posts go live, the most useful voice feedback comes from the audience, not the client. Track saves, comments and clicks by post style. If the short, playful Threads posts consistently outperform the polished ones, that's a voice insight. Write it down.

## A realistic timeline

How long this takes depends on how much material the client has and how fast they respond. A typical split looks like this:

| Stage | Who | Effort |
|---|---|---|
| Collect sources | Client + AM | One email, sent with the contract |
| AI brand intake | Strategist | One working session, mostly reviewing |
| Profile review + Knowledge doc | Strategist | Under an hour for most brands |
| Calibration batch | Creative + client | One feedback round |
| First plan | Strategist | Same day as calibration sign-off |

The big saving comes from not writing a profile from scratch and not doing three rounds of prompt iteration.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Letting the AI invent proof.** Never publish a statistic, testimonial or guarantee the client hasn't approved. Have a human check every claim.
- **One profile for a multi-brand client.** If a client has distinct sub-brands, give each one its own profile.
- **Skipping the "not us" examples.** They do more work than any adjective list.
- **Never updating the profile.** Revisit after rebrands, product launches and every quarter.
